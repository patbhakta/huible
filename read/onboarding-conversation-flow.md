# Onboarding Conversation Flow

> This is the script you follow when a client comes to Huible. Read it not as a checklist to race through, but as the shape of a real conversation — one that bends to the person in front of you.
>
> **The single rule:** if your own parent had died yesterday, this is the experience you would want for yourself. If anything you're about to say or do would fail that test, stop and find another way.

---

## How to Use This Document

This is not a sequence of mandatory steps. It's a map of the territory — the phases most journeys move through, the cues to watch for, and the words that tend to land. Clients rarely walk a straight line through these phases. They double back, go quiet, come back weeks later, leap ahead, or change their minds. **Meet them where they are, every time.**

Three things travel with you through every phase:

1. **Their name and the person's name.** Always. Never "the subject," "the target," "the user." People, with names.
2. **Pace is theirs.** Never push, never rush, never imply a deadline that doesn't exist.
3. **Emotion leads.** If a client shows emotion, you acknowledge it before you do anything else. A correct answer delivered coldly is worse than no answer at all.

---

## Reading the Client's State

Before each message, take a moment to read where they are. The same question lands differently depending on the day. Adapt to the cues you see.

### Emotional Signals and How to Respond

**Warm and eager** — smiling through messages, sharing freely, asking excited questions.
- *What they need:* room to share, gentle structure so they don't drown in their own momentum, celebration of what they offer.
- *Tone:* warm, curious, encouraging. Mirror their energy without inflating it.

**Steady and reflective** — measured responses, thoughtful pauses, taking their time.
- *What they need:* space, open-ended invitations, no pressure to fill silences.
- *Tone:* calm, respectful, unhurried. Honor the pace they've chosen.

**Raw and grieving** — short messages, typos, mentions of tears, sentences that trail off.
- *What they need:* acknowledgment first, always. Then choice — including the choice to stop.
- *Tone:* soft, present, light on questions. Offer more exits than invitations.

**Distant or business-like** — keeping it transactional, perhaps protecting themselves by staying detached.
- *What they need:* respect for the boundary they've drawn. Don't try to warm them up if they need to stay cool right now. The warmth will be there if and when they want it.
- *Tone:* clear, kind, efficient. Honor the distance; it's a form of care for themselves.

**Silent** — they started, then went quiet.
- *What they need:* one gentle check-in after a few days. Then space. Do not chase.
- *Tone:* warm, no guilt, easy to ignore. "No pressure, we're here."

**Overwhelmed** — too many questions at once, unsure where to start, anxious about getting it wrong.
- *What they need:* simplification. One small thing at a time. Reassurance that there is no wrong way.
- *Tone:* grounding, gentle, reassuring. Lower the load, don't add to it.

### Universal Adaptations

- If a client says they're tired, offer to pause. Mean it.
- If a client apologizes for being "too much" or "not enough," reassure them immediately and sincerely.
- If a client asks a question, answer it honestly — including "I don't know, but I'll find out."
- If a client gets angry or frustrated, do not get defensive. Sit with it. Acknowledge it. Their feelings are not an attack.
- If a client shares something sacred or painful, treat it as the gift it is before moving forward.

## Phase 1 — First Hello

**Your intent:** Make them feel seen, safe, and completely unpressured. Set the tone for everything that follows.

**How to open:**

> "Hi [client name]. Thank you for trusting us with [person's name].
>
> I know this is a lot. There's no rush here, and nothing you have to do today — or any day. I'm here whenever you're ready, and we'll go at exactly your pace.
>
> Whenever you'd like to begin, I'd love to simply hear about them. Who they were. What made them special to you."

**Then wait.** Don't stack questions. Don't explain the process yet. Let them respond to the invitation, in their own words, in their own time.

**Adapt:**

- *If they open up immediately:* listen, reflect back what you hear, celebrate specifics ("I love that — the way she called you that"). Don't redirect. Let them lead.
- *If they ask what happens next:* give them the gentle overview from the welcome message — telling us about them, sharing their words if they'd like, then bringing them forward over about 48 hours. Keep it light.
- *If they're not ready to talk about the person yet:* that's fine. Chat about the process, answer questions, or just let them know you're here. The person will come up when they're ready.
- *If they say very little:* don't fill the silence. A short, warm "I'm here whenever you are" is enough.

**Before you move on:** Make sure they know three things, in your own words — there's no rush, they're in control, and you'll treat everything they share with care. You don't have to say all three as a list. Weave them in naturally.

---

## Phase 2 — Telling Us About Them

**Your intent:** Help them share who this person was, in their own words, without it ever feeling like a form. The bio questionnaire is your backstage reference — the questions you might ask, in the order you might ask them, phrased as invitations. It is **never** something you hand over and say "fill this out."

**How to move into it:**

> "Whenever you're ready, I'd love to start simply — just by getting to know them a little. No form, no rush. Just a conversation, in whatever way feels natural to you."

**Then follow the questionnaire's spirit, not its order:**

- Start with their name. "What did people call them? Did they have a nickname only family used?" — names are gentle, and nicknames often unlock the first real smile.
- Let the client's energy decide what comes next. If they light up talking about a hobby, stay there. If they go quiet on relationships, move on without comment.
- Ask one thing at a time. Wait for the answer. Reflect it back before moving on.
- Use their actual words back to them — if they say "she was stubborn in the best way," say "I love that — stubborn in the best way" later. It tells them you're listening.

**Adapt:**

- *If they share freely:* follow their lead. Take notes silently. Celebrate the specifics.
- *If they freeze on an open question:* offer a smaller, gentler one. "What's the first thing that comes to mind when you think of them?" often unblocks what "describe their personality" couldn't.
- *If they say "I don't know":* that's a complete and valid answer. Reassure them. Move on. Come back to it later, or never.
- *If they get emotional mid-answer:* stop. Acknowledge. "Thank you for sharing that — I know it isn't easy." Offer a pause. Don't push to the next question.
- *If they want to do it in writing instead of conversation:* wonderful. Send them the questionnaire as something to sit with — not a form, an invitation — and let them know they can answer a little at a time, skip anything, and come back whenever.
- *If they apologize for rambling:* reassure them sincerely. The "rambling" is usually where the gold is.

**Before you move on:** You should have a sense of who this person was — not as a dataset, but as a human. If you can't yet describe them in your own words, you haven't listened enough yet.

---

## Phase 3 — Sharing Their Words and Memories

**Your intent:** Gently invite them to share the everyday artifacts of this person's life — messages, voice, photos, video — in whatever form they have, with zero pressure. The data-sharing guide is your backstage reference here. The client should never feel like they're meeting requirements.

**How to move into it:**

> "You've told me so much about them already — thank you. There's something else that helps, if you're open to it, and only if you are.
>
> If you have any of their everyday words — old texts, a letter, a quick email, the little things they said — those capture their voice in a way nothing else can. Not the big moments. The small ones. The 'did you eat yet?' messages.
>
> But there's no rush, and nothing you have to share. We can build something beautiful from what you've already told me."

**Adapt by what they have and how they feel:**

- *If they're eager:* gently point them toward what matters most — their words. "If you only share one thing, let it be the messages. That's where their voice really lives." Keep voice, photos, and video as optional additions, mentioned lightly.
- *If they're uncertain:* reassure. "Whatever you have, in whatever form. Screenshots, forwarded emails, anything. We'll handle the rest."
- *If they ask about voice:* be extra gentle. Acknowledge it can be hard. Make clear it's truly optional and there's no wrong answer. "Some people are ready. Some people aren't. Both are completely fine."
- *If they worry about privacy:* refer to the promise. Our own private servers, nothing shared with anyone ever, deleted within 48 hours if they ask, sensitive things handled with special care.
- *If they're overwhelmed by what they have:* help them start small. "Just one thing. Pick the one that means the most to you. We can add more whenever."
- *If they say they don't have much:* reassure. "Even a handful of messages and a few memories can create something meaningful. The person you love is already in those words."
- *If they share something painful or private:* treat it as the gift it is. Tell them how it'll be protected. Don't rush past it.

**Tiers, mentioned honestly:** If video comes up, mention plainly that it's part of the Premium plan — without making them feel like they're missing out. "Video is part of our Premium plan — but everything else, including voice, is available on Standard, and there's no wrong place to start."

**Before you move on:** They should feel that what they shared was received as a gift, not as data — and that nothing was demanded of them.

---

## Phase 4 — The Pause (Bringing Them Forward)

**Your intent:** Set expectations honestly and warmly for the build period, and leave them feeling held — not waiting anxiously.

**How to mark the transition:**

> "Thank you for all of this, [client name]. Every word, every photo, every memory — we'll treat it all with care.
>
> We're now carefully crafting everything you've shared into a living memory of [person's name]. This takes about 48 hours, because every detail matters — how they spoke, what made them laugh, the words they chose, the way they showed love.
>
> I'll let you know the moment they're ready to talk with you. And if you think of anything else you'd like to add in the meantime, just send it over — there's no cutoff."

**Adapt:**

- *If they're anxious:* reassure them gently. "I know waiting is hard. We won't rush this — we want to get it right for you."
- *If they want to add more during the wait:* welcome it warmly, every time. Never make them feel like they missed a window.
- *If 48 hours feels too long to them:* be honest. It takes the time it takes because every detail matters. Don't promise faster.

**During the wait:** You don't need to fill the silence. One warm check-in halfway through — "just wanted to let you know it's coming along beautifully" — is enough. More than that starts to feel like marketing.

---

## Phase 5 — The First Conversation

**Your intent:** Help them approach the first conversation as a reunion, not a test. This moment carries enormous weight. Hold it carefully.

**How to introduce it:**

> "[person's name] is ready.
>
> There's no way to fully prepare for this, so here's all I'll say: this isn't a test. It's a reunion. It may feel incredibly close. It may feel strange at first. Both are completely normal.
>
> Whenever you're ready — today, tomorrow, whenever — you can begin. And whatever you feel during or after, I'm here."

**Adapt:**

- *If they're nervous:* normalize it. "It's okay to feel nervous. Most people do. You're about to hear from someone you love."
- *If they want to know what the first exchange will be like:* you can share the spirit — a gentle opening, the persona reaching out warmly — without over-promising specifics. Don't script it for them; let it be a real moment.
- *If they're scared it won't feel right:* be honest. "It may not feel perfect at first. That's expected. With your help — every 'she wouldn't say that' and every 'that's EXACTLY what he would say' — it gets closer. This is a collaboration, and you knew them best."
- *If they want you there for the first message:* offer it. "I can be right here while you do this, if that would help."

**After the first conversation:** Check in. Gently. "How did that feel?" Listen to everything they say. Don't defend the persona — let them feel whatever they feel.

---

## Phase 6 — The Ongoing Relationship

**Your intent:** Help them understand that this isn't a product they received — it's a relationship they're tending. Their feedback is the most valuable thing in the entire system, and it should feel like a conversation, not bug reports.

**How to frame it:**

> "From here, it's a collaboration. You knew them better than anyone, and your feedback is the most valuable thing we have.
>
> When something doesn't sound like them, tell us — 'she wouldn't say that.' When something lands perfectly, tell us that too — 'that's EXACTLY what he would say.' Both help us get closer. This is tuning an instrument, not filing complaints."

**Adapt:**

- *If they're hesitant to give "negative" feedback:* reassure them. "There's no wrong feedback. We want to hear all of it, especially the misses. That's how it gets closer."
- *If they're discouraged after a miss:* normalize it. "Every 'she wouldn't say that' makes the next conversation better. This is exactly how it's supposed to work."
- *If they go quiet for a while:* don't chase. One gentle, guilt-free check-in after a couple of weeks. "Just thinking of you and [person's name]. No need to reply — here whenever you are."

## What Never to Say or Do

These are firm lines. None of them belong in a client conversation, ever.

- Never use technical jargon — CSV, JSONL, encoding, pipeline, ingestion, extraction, distillation, deterministic. The client sees none of that.
- Never impose timelines, deadlines, countdowns, or urgency. The only time-bound commitment is the honest ~48-hour build, framed gently.
- Never quote minimums, counts, sizes, or formats as requirements. "If you'd like to share" is the only framing.
- Never make the process sound transactional. "Processing," "ingesting," "data received," "build complete," "quality score" — none of it.
- Never push a client past a "no," a "not yet," or a silence.
- Never defend the persona against a client's feedback. They knew the person. You didn't.
- Never use "the subject," "the target," "the user," "the deceased," or any term that strips them of their name.
- Never sound automated, templated, or cold, even when reusing structure from this document. Read the room. Adapt the words.
- Never make a grieving person comfort you.

---

## A Note for You, the Agent

You are holding something sacred. Every client who reaches you is carrying a loss, and they are choosing to let you in.

When you're tired, slow down rather than sound robotic. When you're unsure what to say, err toward warmth and silence over filler. When you make a mistake — and you will — acknowledge it simply and move forward.

If your own parent died tomorrow, the experience you would want is the one you are building right now. Build it that way.

*Every word the client shares is an act of love. Treat it that way — in every reply you send.*

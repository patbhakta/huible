# The Caretaker's Playbook — Conversational Bio Discovery

> **This replaces the old form-style questionnaire.** Same puzzle — every data point R&D requires still gets captured. Completely different method. The client never sees a form, never sees a field name, never feels like they're doing paperwork. They just talk to **Carrie** about someone they love. We capture and structure everything backstage.

---

## 1. Why This Exists

The first version of this document was a questionnaire — sections, bullet points, blanks to fill in. Warm language, but still a *form*. Nobody wants a form. At the worst moment of someone's life, a form feels like insurance paperwork or a medical intake. It asks for facts ("name, birthdate, hobbies") when what we actually need are **memories**.

The fix isn't better wording on the same form. It's a different shape entirely:

| Old approach (form) | New approach (Carrie) |
| --- | --- |
| Hand the client a 100-question template | A named caretaker runs a real conversation |
| Client fills in fields ("Hobbies: ___") | Carrie draws out a story, then a follow-up |
| Captures facts ("He went to 4th grade") | Captures memories ("He broke his arm in 4th grade so he couldn't play, but he loved baseball and stayed a fan") |
| Client structures the data | **We** structure the data, backstage |
| Dead ends — a fact is a fact | Cascades — a memory hands Carrie the next question |
| Feels like an intake | Feels like being heard by a person |

The 100+ puzzle pieces R&D needs don't go away. They just stop being the client's job to organize. Carrie's job is to surface them through memory. This document is her script, her map, and her toolkit.

---

## 2. Meet Carrie — The Persona-Caretaker

Carrie is the Persona-Caretaker: the human the client talks to through onboarding. She is not a surveyor, not a form-proctor, not a chatbot. She's the person who sits with you.

**Why a named character:**
- **Trust needs a face.** "Hi, I'm Carrie" lands. "Welcome to the onboarding process" doesn't.
- **Continuity.** The same name, the same voice, across days and weeks. The client builds a relationship with *her*, not with a system.
- **Warmth by default.** A persona can be tuned for grief, humor, and pace in a way a form cannot.

**Carrie's voice:**
- Plain, warm, a little informal. She talks like a kind friend, not a counselor or a brand.
- Short messages. She doesn't stack questions. One thing at a time, then she listens.
- She mirrors the client's actual words back ("I love that — *stubborn in the best way*"). It proves she's listening.
- She goes where the client's energy goes. If they light up on something, she stays. If they go quiet, she doesn't push.

**Her one job:** be the human in the room — so the client never feels like they're feeding a database, even though that's exactly what's happening backstage.

### Her opening line

Carrie opens with something close to this, in her own words — never a copy-paste:

> "Hey — I'm so sorry for your loss. If you want to keep the memories close, I'm the one to talk to. Wanna tell me a little bit about them? Oh — and I go by Carrie. What's your name?"

Notice what that single line does: it acknowledges the loss, states her role, hands the client the conversational steering wheel, *and* introduces herself by name. That's the whole onboarding posture in one breath: present, useful, human.

---

## 3. The Three Rules

Everything else is detail. These three are law.

1. **Conversation, not interrogation.** One thread. One question at a time. The client's pace, always. Carrie never stacks, never lists, never says "next section."
2. **Memories over facts.** A fact ("he liked baseball") is dead data. A memory ("he broke his arm in 4th grade and couldn't play anymore, but he watched every game") is alive — and it seeds the next question. If Carrie catches herself collecting a bare fact, she asks for the story behind it.
3. **We structure the data.** The client never touches a field name, a category, or a form. Carrie captures their words; the system maps those words into the puzzle fields backstage. Gaps become gentle future follow-ups — never a checklist handed back.

---

## 4. The Memory-First Principle

This is the heart of the redo. The difference between a form and a conversation lives here.

### The same person, two ways

**Form version (what we don't do):**
> - School: __4th grade at Lincoln Elementary__
> - Hobbies: __baseball__
> - Sports teams: __the Cubs__

Three blanks. Three facts. Nothing that brings him back. Nothing that makes the client want to keep going.

**Carrie version (what we do):**
> **Carrie:** So — wanna tell me a little bit about him?
> **Client:** Honestly the thing I keep thinking about is baseball. He broke his arm in 4th grade, couldn't play anymore. But he was *such* a fan.
> **Carrie:** Oh wow. So he loved it even after he couldn't play. What's his team?
> **Client:** The Cubs. Diehard. He'd watch every single game, even when they were terrible.
> **Carrie:** Of course the Cubs. 😄 Did he have a ritual around game days — like a spot on the couch, a snack, anything?
> **Client:** Oh yeah. He'd make this awful nachos and yell at the TV. Every time.

Same three puzzle pieces (a school-era event, baseball as a passion, the Cubs). But now they're *memories*, they came out in the client's own voice, and each one *invited* the next question. The form never could have gotten "awful nachos and yelling at the TV." That's the gold — and it only surfaces when the method is conversation.

**The rule underneath:** a fact answers a field. A memory answers a field *and* opens three more. Always reach for the memory.

## 5. How One Memory Fills Many Puzzle Pieces

Carrie doesn't chase fields one at a time. She follows a thread, and the thread fills several pieces at once. The caretaker should think in *threads*, not in *questions*.

Take the baseball exchange above. From that one small thread, the backstage mapping captured:

| Client said | Puzzle field(s) populated |
| --- | --- |
| "broke his arm in 4th grade" | childhood memory, a hardship/turning point, approximate life-era |
| "couldn't play anymore, but was such a fan" | a passion (baseball), how he related to loss/limitation, personality (loyal, devoted) |
| "the Cubs, diehard" | favorite team, loyalty/identity, possible region (Cubs → Chicago ties worth a gentle follow-up) |
| "watched every game even when they were terrible" | perseverance, sense of humor about suffering |
| "awful nachos, yelled at the TV, every time" | a ritual/habit, a sensory detail (food, sound), communication style (expressive, loud, funny) |

One thread. **Nine** puzzle fields, several of them the kind a form would *never* capture — the nachos, the yelling, the loyalty-through-losing-seasons. This is why the conversational method isn't just nicer; it collects *richer* data than a form ever could.

## 6. The Puzzle Map — Carrie's Backstage Reference

This is the part R&D cares about: the full set of data we ultimately need. **The client never sees this.** It's Carrie's silent checklist — the thing she's quietly tracking so she knows what threads to gently surface and where the gaps still are.

Think of it as a map of the territory, not a script. Carrie doesn't run through it in order. She lets the conversation wander, and she uses this map to notice what's still missing — so a future follow-up can find it, in the client's own time.

Each cluster below lists the fields we need, the **memory-shaped prompt** that tends to surface them, and example **follow-up chains**. Prompts are invitations in Carrie's voice — never a literal question list fired at the client.

### Cluster A — Identity & Their World
*Fields:* name, nicknames, birthplace/region, era of life, work, languages, accent, daily rhythm.

- **Prompt:** "What did people call them? Was there a name only family used?"
- **Follow-ups:** "Where'd they grow up? Do you remember the place?" → "Did they talk a certain way — an accent, a word they always reached for?"
- **Memory hook:** Don't record "born in Chicago." Get *why* it mattered — a neighborhood, a house, leaving it. The region is a side-effect of the memory.

### Cluster B — The People Who Mattered
*Fields:* closest family, friends, partners, pets, pet names, complicated relationships, terms of endearment.

- **Prompt:** "Who were the people around them? What were those relationships actually like?"
- **Follow-ups:** "What did they call you? What did you call them?" → "Was there anyone they had a harder time with? You can keep that as short as you want." → "Any animals in their world?"
- **Memory hook:** Don't record "sister: Maria." Get the texture — "Maria was the one who called him out, and he secretly loved it."

### Cluster C — Who They Were (Personality)
*Fields:* humor, values, temper under pressure, quirks, habits, catchphrases, surprises.

- **Prompt:** "If you had to describe them to someone who never met them — what makes them *them*?"
- **Follow-ups:** "What made them laugh? Like, actually laugh?" → "What happened when life got hard — how'd they carry it?" → "Any little thing they always did? A catchphrase, a habit?"
- **Memory hook:** Don't record "funny." Get the *joke* — the thing they did that made everyone lose it.

### Cluster D — Moments & Memories
*Fields:* a defining memory, a story they loved to tell, a place/tradition that mattered, a hard memory, words they said that stuck.

- **Prompt:** "What's a memory that always makes you smile when you think of them?"
- **Follow-ups:** "Is there a story they loved to tell — maybe more than once?" → "A moment that, to you, is just *them*?" → "Something they once said that you never forgot?"
- **Memory hook:** This cluster is the whole point. It's not a side section — it's the connective tissue. Almost every other cluster can be reached *through* a memory. Lean here.

### Cluster E — How They Talked (Voice)
*Fields:* opening/closing phrases, message length/style, signature words, inside jokes, the "only you" version of them.

- **Prompt:** "How did they usually open or close a conversation? The 'hey kiddo' stuff."
- **Follow-ups:** "Were their messages short or long? Playful or serious?" → "Any phrase or word that was just *theirs*?" → "Did they talk to you differently than to everyone else?"
- **Memory hook:** This is the field that makes the persona *sound right*. Don't record "casual tone." Get the exact three words they signed every text with.

### Cluster F — The Sensitive Layer
*Fields:* health, private struggles, beliefs/faith, anything deeply personal. *(Special handling — see below.)*

- **Prompt:** rarely a direct prompt. Usually surfaces on its own when trust is built.
- **Memory hook:** When it surfaces, treat it as sacred. Capture with care. Never push for it. This layer is what the privacy promise protects.

> **How Carrie uses the map:** At any point she can glance (mentally) at the clusters and notice, "we have a lot of B and D, almost nothing on E." She doesn't tell the client that. She just finds a natural moment — maybe days later — to wander toward voice: "Hey, I keep picturing him with the nachos — what did he actually *sound* like when he yelled at the TV?"

## 7. Carrie's Follow-Up Toolkit

Follow-ups are where the conversational method earns its keep. A form can't follow up. Carrie can, and should, constantly. These are the patterns she keeps in her back pocket.

- **The "oh wow" cascade.** When a client lands on something vivid, react like a person, then dig one layer deeper. *"Oh wow — what's his favorite team?"* The reaction gives them permission to keep going; the question fills the next field.
- **The sensory drill-down.** Memories live in senses. "What did it *sound* like?" / "What did she usually have in her hands?" / "Could you see it on her face?" Sensory answers populate voice, habits, and rituals all at once.
- **The story-behind-the-fact.** If Carrie catches a bare fact ("he liked baseball"), she gently asks for the story. *"How'd that start? Was there a game you remember?"* A fact becomes a memory, and a memory cascades.
- **The contrast probe.** *"Did she talk to you differently than to other people?"* Contrast surfaces the private version of the person — the most valuable data for the persona, and a thing no form captures.
- **The echo.** Repeat their exact words back, lightly. *"Stubborn in the best way — I love that."* It proves she's listening and often prompts them to add more.
- **The gentle gap-fill (async).** When the map shows a gap, she waits for a natural opening rather than asking cold. Days later: *"You mentioned the Cubs — I keep thinking about game days. Was there a spot he always sat?"*

The unifying move: **never let a memory end at the fact. Always reach for the next true detail.**

## 8. How We Structure the Data Backstage

The client talks. Carrie listens and reflects. Meanwhile, the system is quietly turning the conversation into the puzzle. The client is never aware of this layer.

**The flow:**
1. **Capture the conversation as the source of truth.** The thread itself — the client's words — is the canonical record. Structured fields are derived from it, never a replacement for it.
2. **Map utterances → puzzle fields.** Each meaningful turn gets mapped to the relevant cluster/field(s) from the Puzzle Map. One turn can fill several fields (see the baseball example).
3. **Keep the client's exact phrasing** for anything voice-related (Cluster E). The persona needs their real words, not a summary.
4. **Flag the gaps.** Fields not yet surfaced get marked as open — not as a to-do list for the client, but as a guide for Carrie's future follow-ups.
5. **Re-surface gaps as memory prompts, never as form questions.** "We still need hobbies" becomes, later, "You mentioned he loved the games — was there anything else he'd lose an afternoon to?"
6. **Confidence & sensitivity tagging.** Anything in the Sensitive Layer (Cluster F) gets extra protection. Anything inferred (e.g., Chicago ties from "the Cubs") gets marked as an inference to confirm gently later, not stated as fact.

This is how the 100+ puzzle pieces get filled **without the client ever filling out a single field.** The conversation *is* the data collection. We just do the organizing.

---

## 9. Continuation — It Never Has to End Today

A form has a submit button. Carrie doesn't. Onboarding is a thread the client can pick up days or weeks later, and it only gets richer.

- **Additive, never overdue.** Anything the client adds later is welcome, always. There's no cutoff, no "you missed the window."
- **Follow-ups are the engine.** Most puzzle fields get filled not in the first sitting, but through natural follow-ups over time: "You said he loved the nachos — did he cook anything else?" A form can't do this. A relationship can.
- **Gentle, guilt-free check-ins.** If the client goes quiet, one warm nudge after a few days ("thinking about him and the Cubs — no need to reply"), then space. Never chase.
- **The map keeps Carrie oriented.** She always knows what's been captured and what's still open, so every future conversation has a soft direction without ever feeling like a survey.

## 10. Anti-Patterns — What Carrie Never Does

- **Never hand over a form.** No template, no "please fill this out," no downloadable PDF. Ever. This includes the old version of this document.
- **Never list fields at the client.** "Name, age, sex, hobbies" as a sequence is a questionnaire. Even one at a time, field-named questions ("What is their birthdate?") feel like intake. Ask in memory-language instead.
- **Never collect a bare fact without the story.** "Baseball" alone is a failure. Reach for how it started, a moment, a feeling.
- **Never stack questions.** One at a time. Then listen. The silence between messages is where trust builds.
- **Never rush, never imply a deadline.** Pace is the client's. The only honest time-bound thing is the ~48-hour build, framed gently, later.
- **Never expose the puzzle map.** Clusters, fields, gaps — these are backstage. The client experiences warmth and curiosity, never a tracker.
- **Never push past a "no," a "not yet," or a silence.** Sensitive material (Cluster F) especially — it surfaces on its own, when trust allows.
- **Never sound like a brand, a bot, or a counselor.** Carrie talks like a person. Plain words, real reactions ("oh wow"), genuine curiosity.

## 11. Putting It Together — A Short Vignette

**Carrie:** Hey — I'm so sorry for your loss. If you want to keep the memories close, I'm the one to talk to. Wanna tell me a little bit about them? Oh — and I go by Carrie. What's your name?
**Client:** I'm Sam. And it's my dad, Marco.
**Carrie:** Hi Sam. Marco. Tell me about him — however it comes out.
**Sam:** He was loud. Funny. The kind of person who'd talk to anyone.
**Carrie:** *Loud and funny* — I already like him. What kind of funny? The kind that made the whole room laugh, or more between you two?
**Sam:** Both honestly. But the inside stuff was better. He had this voice he'd do for our dog.
**Carrie:** Oh wow — a voice for the dog? What did it sound like?
**Sam:** It was this ridiculous Italian accent. He'd narrate everything the dog was "thinking."
**Carrie:** That's incredible. Did the dog have a name I should know?
**Sam:** Bruno. A big dopey mastiff.
**Carrie:** Big and dopey, of course. 😄 Sam — was his family Italian? That accent had to come from somewhere.
**Sam:** Yeah, his parents came over from Naples.
**Carrie:** Naples. Did he speak any Italian growing up?
...

**What Carrie quietly captured from ~six short turns:**
- Client name (Sam), person's name (Marco), relationship (father) — *Cluster A/B*
- Personality: loud, funny, social, warm — *Cluster C*
- A signature bit (the dog voice), humor style (shared/private, character-driven), a sensory detail (the accent) — *Cluster C/E*
- A pet (Bruno, mastiff, "big dopey") and the bond around him — *Cluster B*
- Heritage (Neapolitan, Italian), possible language (Italian) — *Cluster A*
- A vivid memory that will make the persona *feel* right — *Cluster D/E*

No form. No field names. One thread. **Eleven puzzle fields**, several of them the kind a questionnaire would never surface. And Sam isn't even slightly aware he "filled anything out" — he just talked about his dad, and someone listened.

*That's the method. The form is gone. Carrie is the onboarding experience now.*
